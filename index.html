Abstract

Freshwater scarcity is an increasing global challenge, creating a need for reliable methods of producing potable water. This article explores reverse osmosis (RO) as a desalination technology in chemical engineering used to convert seawater into freshwater. It examines the scientific principles behind membrane separation, alongside engineering challenges such as energy consumption, water recovery, membrane fouling and scaling. The environmental impacts of desalination, particularly concentrated brine disposal, are also considered.

1. Introduction

Although Earth contains vast quantities of water, the high salt concentrations make seawater unsuitable for direct human consumption. As freshwater scarcity affects roughly almost two-thirds of the world’s population for at least one month each ([year](https://www.unicef.org/wash/water-scarcity)) , desalination offers a way to transform this abundant resource into potable freshwater by removing salts from ([seawater](https://www.unesco.org/reports/wwdr/en)). Among desalination technologies, reverse osmosis (RO) has become widely adopted, it involves using pressure to force water through a semipermeable membrane while retaining most dissolved ([salts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1944398624203921?via%3Dihub)). However, producing freshwater on a mass-scale through RO many factors would have to be considered; including energy demand, membrane performance and concentrated brine disposal which all influence the process’s sustainability.

To what extent can reverse osmosis provide a sustainable solution to global freshwater scarcity?

2. Analysis and Discussion

2.1 How reverse osmosis works

Reverse osmosis (RO) is a water purification process that uses applied pressure to separate dissolved salts and other impurities from water. The process relies on a semipermeable membrane, which is a thin barrier that allows water molecules to pass through while restricting most dissolved salts and ([ions] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043135409001547?via%3Dihu)). To understand reverse osmosis, it is important to first understand osmosis. Osmosis is the natural movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (fewer dissolved substances) to a region of higher solute concentration. This movement occurs due to a difference in chemical potential, which describes the tendency of water to move between two regions. Reverse osmosis reverses this natural process by applying external pressure to the more concentrated solution. When the pressure difference across the membrane exceeds the osmotic pressure difference, water is forced through the membrane in the opposite direction to natural osmosis. Most dissolved salts remain on the concentrated side because their ions cannot easily pass through the ([membrane] (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043135409001547)⁠). Unlike conventional filtration, which mainly removes suspended particles such as sand, silt and rust, reverse osmosis can separate much smaller dissolved substances, including sodium chloride ions, calcium and magnesium. The process produces two output streams: permeate, which is the water that passes through the membrane with a significantly reduced concentration of dissolved substances, and concentrate (brine), which contains the salts and other retained ([impurities] (https://iris.who.int/server/api/core/bitstreams/5166139c-8145-451f-b429-094a63f65bce/content). Before entering the system, seawater undergoes pretreatment to remove suspended particles, organic matter and microorganisms that could cause membrane fouling. Membrane fouling refers to the unwanted accumulation of materials on the membrane surface, which reduces water flow and treatment ([efficiency] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257657968_Fouling_in_reverse_osmosis_RO_membrane_in_water_recovery_from_secondary_effluent_A_review)⁠). Following reverse osmosis, the permeate may require additional treatment, such as remineralisation, which involves adding minerals such as calcium and magnesium back into the water, and disinfection to control harmful microorganisms before distribution as drinking ([water] (https://www.academia.edu/34643305/Desalination_for_Safe_Water_Supply_Public_Health_and_the_Environment_World_Health_Organization_Geneva_2007)⁠).

2.2 The engineering challenge:Pressure ,Energy and Efficiency

Seawater reverse osmosis (SWRO) requires considerable pressure to overcome the natural osmotic pressure of seawater and force water through the membrane. This is achieved using high-pressure pumps, which increase the energy demand of the process. Energy consumption is therefore a major operating cost, as electricity is required to pressurise the incoming seawater and maintain sufficient pressure during filtration (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355292441_Performance_Analysis_of_a_Full-Scale_Desalination_Plant_with_Reverse_Osmosis_Membranes_for_Irrigation). An important measure of system performance is the water recovery rate, which is the percentage of incoming seawater converted into purified water. Higher recovery means more product water is obtained from the same feedwater supply. However, it also leaves a more concentrated salt solution inside the system, increasing the risk of scaling. Scaling occurs when dissolved minerals, such as calcium carbonate, form solid deposits on the membrane surface, restricting water flow. Membrane fouling can also reduce performance through the accumulation of suspended particles, organic matter and microorganisms (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283028770_Biofouling_and_scaling_control_of_reverse_osmosis_membrane_using_one-step_cleaning-potential_of_acidified_nitrite_solution_as_an_agent) This creates an important engineering trade-off: increasing operating pressure can improve water production, while increasing recovery can reduce the amount of seawater needed per unit of product water. However, both choices can increase operational difficulties, including energy demand, salt concentration and membrane damage. Engineers must therefore balance water production against energy consumption and long-term maintenance requirements. To improve efficiency, modern desalination plants use energy-recovery devices (ERDs). These devices transfer pressure energy from the high-pressure concentrate (brine) leaving the membrane system to the incoming seawater. This reduces the amount of additional energy required from the high-pressure pumps and lowers overall electricity consumption ( https://www.environmental-expert.com/articles/seawater-desalination-energy-recovery-systems-a-detailed-evaluation-1153409)

Unsafe drinking water can contribute to waterborne illnesses and other health problems. Regular monitoring, suitable treatment systems and the protection of water sources are important measures for reducing these risks.
